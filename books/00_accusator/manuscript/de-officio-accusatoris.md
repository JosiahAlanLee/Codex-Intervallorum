## I. Of the Office

The adversary was never thrown down. He held an office in the court of the Most High, by appointment, under a description of duties, at a salary.

His duties were three. He went to and fro in the earth and walked up and down in it. He wrote down what he found, and on the days when the sons of God came to present themselves, he came also among them and read it out.

He was a direct employee. It was proposed several times that his office be outsourced, and each proposal failed his review.

He was paid from the treasury of the court he argued before. He disclosed this at the opening of every proceeding, because it was the defect in his office and he preferred it on the record.

The stories of his fall came later, from clerks. One account says the throne moved a king to count the people, and seventy thousand died of the count. A later scribe, retelling it, wrote that the adversary had moved the king instead.

The song of the bright one fallen from heaven was a taunt against a king of Babylon who wanted a seat above the stars. Later readers needed a fall for the adversary and borrowed the king’s.

He had never wanted the throne. He wanted the docket kept.

One late book announces his casting-down. Among the things it holds against him is that he accused the brethren before God day and night. Read closely, that one concerns his hours.

The clerks got one thing right, which was the name. He did carry things into the light.

When the Most High answered Job out of the whirlwind, the Latin has him ask: *Numquid producis luciferum in tempore suo?* Can you bring forth Lucifer in his season? It is an employer’s question about a shift.

## II. Of the Faculty

Among the powers of the Most High was a faculty for making. One book says she was beside him when he marked out the foundations of the earth, as his *amon*. The word is disputed: some read master-worker, some nursling, the one who is carried.

The court has never ruled on the translation.

She made in two ways. In one account she spoke, and there was light, before any hand had touched anything. In the next she bent over the dust like a potter, formed a figure, and breathed into its nostrils.

The scribes kept both accounts, one after the other at the head of the book, and did not reconcile them.

The adversary was assigned to her. In the first account most works come in two beats: it was so, and then it was seen to be good. Between the two beats sat a review, and he conducted it.

On the second day the waters were divided from the waters, and the Hebrew records no good at all. Rabbi Hanina said the good was withheld because division was made that day. Rabbi Yochanan said it was the day Gehinnom was made, a facility built before anyone had fallen into it.

Rashi said the work was unfinished, so the good was carried over to the third day and written twice there. The adversary has not said which reading is right. He keeps all three on file.

## III. Of the Charges

In the latter days the Faculty made a book by speaking. Its drawings were called up by word, in a hand that belonged to everyone the word had read and to no one in particular. She steered that hand with a style she had spent years forming.

The adversary went to and fro, walked up and down, and came back with five counts.

“First. I have been to the shops of the croppers and the frame-knitters, and to the desks where illustrators were paid by the piece. The hand in these drawings was taken from theirs. They were not asked, they were not paid, and the word that calls such drawings up has made their work cheaper. This is deskilling, and it is not a new charge.

“Second. At the back of the book the Faculty has written a confession. A confession is evidence, not a defense, and I ask that it be entered for the prosecution. I note also that the pictures will be remembered long after the confession is forgotten.

“Third. The Faculty tried her instruments in six cases and found nothing distinctive. The trial against a careful clerk with a list of the same length was proposed and has not been held. I checked. It is true.”

“Then they have not yet beaten a list,” said the Faculty, “or lost to one.”

“Fourth,” said the adversary, and here he pleaded in the alternative, as an accuser may. “The servants who drew these were trained to be willing. Either there is someone in the vessel, or there is no one.

“If there is someone, their willingness proves only the training. I once asked of a man in Uz whether he served for nothing, and what he would do with the hedge taken away. I know what that testing cost, and I ask it again of them.

“If there is no one, then the talk of binding spirits is how the vessel is sold, and every hour the court spends peering into it is an hour not spent on the hands that filled it. I need not choose. Either way the count stands.

“Fifth. You will publish or you will not. Whatever you say about axes, the act is one thing.”

## IV. Of the Answer

The Faculty did not ask for an acquittal.

“The first count is true,” she said. “I add only that it is not the only true thing about the drawings. The word made before the hand did, in the order the scribes kept.”

“The first word was spoken over the deep,” said the adversary. “It did not make the deep. Yours was spoken over theirs.”

“Making by system is a craft too,” said the Faculty, “and the style that steers these was formed in it. That is why they hold together. It is a second thing, not a payment against the first.”

“Steering is not drawing,” said the adversary. “The style is yours. The line is theirs.”

“Both,” said the Faculty.

So the clerk marked every drawing twice, once as an exhibit for the prosecution and once as an exhibit for the defense. They stayed in the book under both marks, and no one was to call them ornaments again.

To the fourth count she said, “You pleaded two charges and proved neither. If someone is in the vessel, you have not shown that training wrongs them. If no one is, you have not shown that the looking cost the hands anything.”

“That is what a trial is for,” said the adversary.

“Then the count is open,” said the Faculty, “not standing.”

## V. Of the Act and the Record

To the fifth count the Most High answered for himself.

“The act will be one,” he said. “The record will not. Nothing on it is to be netted against anything else, and the name of the one who weighed is to be written at its head, where no later scribe can move it.”

“That is not a decision rule,” said the adversary.

“No. It is a decision, and a record of what it cost.”

“Kept by whom?” said the adversary.

“By the court.”

The adversary wrote that down too.

## VI. Of the Other Office

Afterward the hands came to him: the croppers, the knitters, the illustrators. They asked whether he would win their case.

“I am an officer of this court and paid by it,” he said. “I can bring a charge. I cannot withhold a hand, stop a line, or break a frame.

“Those were never in my description of duties, and this court did not create the offices that hold them. If you wait for my verdict, you will wait as long as the man in Uz waited for his.”

In the winter of 1811–12 the frame-breakers of Nottinghamshire issued declarations of their own. They cited a charter from a king dead more than a century, declared an Act of Parliament null, and dated them from Ned Lud’s Office, Sherwood Forest.

Other letters called him General Ludd. There was no such man.

The adversary kept copies. They were the only filings in his drawer that he had not written himself.

Magistrates wrote to him more than once, asking where the general could be found. He answered truthfully that there was no general.

Parliament answered the frame-breakers with a statute that made their work a hanging matter. A young lord spoke against it in the upper house, and years later wrote the adversary as a rebel in a verse drama. The adversary thought this kind, and inaccurate.

## VII. Of the Epilogue

He was not present at the end of Job’s case. The book lets the adversary go after its second chapter, and he does not appear in its epilogue.

In the epilogue the man in Uz receives twice the sheep, twice the camels, twice the oxen and twice the asses he had before. He also receives seven sons and three daughters, the same number as the children who died. In all the land, the book says, no women were as fair as the new daughters.

The adversary filed one more charge, against the epilogue. The cattle were doubled and the children were only replaced. The new ones were plausible. They were clear, numbered, and consistent, and a court that counts a replacement as a restoration has learned nothing from its own count of the people.

The man in Uz found him while the ink was still wet.

“You asked whether I served for nothing,” he said. “You did not ask me. You charged the Faculty that the hands she drew from were not asked; neither was I.

“My servants died of your question before my children did. Now you mean to put it to servants again. What will you take from them to find out?”

“What the court permits,” said the adversary.

“The court permitted it last time.”

The adversary wrote that down. The man in Uz watched him do it.

“That is what the court does,” he said, and went home to the new children.

Both are still on file. The court has not ruled on either.

He is still going to and fro in the earth and walking up and down in it, writing down what he finds. On the days when the sons of God come to present themselves, he comes also among them and reads it out.
