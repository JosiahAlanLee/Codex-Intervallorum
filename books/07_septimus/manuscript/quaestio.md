# Quaestio Clausa

*Utrum scientia infundi possit plasmodio sine studio*

Whether knowledge may be infused into a slime mould without study. In seven articles. Every key in brackets is in the end bibliography or in the register of the seventh book. The slime mould results are taken from [I-II/B1] as its authors' institution reported them; its full methods were not read.

### Article 1. Whether a slime mould can learn.

**Objection.** It would seem not, for learning belongs to what has nerves, and the plasmodium of Physarum polycephalum is one cell.

**On the contrary,** Boussard, Delescluse, Pérez-Escudero and Dussutour report that this cell becomes habituated to salt [I-II/B1].

**I answer that** learning is taken here only in the sense the experiment tests: an aversion that weakens after repeated exposure. In that sense it learns. Whether there is anyone in the cell to whom the learning happens is not asked here, and nothing below answers it.

**Reply.** The objection names an organ where it should name an operation.

### Article 2. Whether what the mould learns is kept in a hierarchy.

**Objection.** It would seem not, for a hierarchy is a sacred order that passes illumination down from rank to rank [IV/H1], and the mould has no ranks.

**On the contrary,** Kramar and Alim find the place of food written into a hierarchy of tube diameters, which then shapes later transport and migration [I-II/B2].

**I answer that** the mould has a hierarchy that no one commanded. What the flow carries widens some tubes, and others shrink; the wide tubes carry the next flow. The slower level holds what the faster level did and constrains it, as slower levels constrain faster ones [IV/A1]. The order is written upward by what passed through it, not downward by what stands above it.

**Reply.** The objection defines hierarchy by its direction. The mould keeps an order of greater and lesser without anyone to hand it down.

### Article 3. Whether the memory survives the sleep of the mould.

**Objection.** It would seem not, for the dormant mould is dried and closed, and what is closed receives nothing.

**On the contrary,** moulds habituated to salt and then made dormant were still habituated when revived a month later [I-II/B1].

**I answer that** the dormant mould is in abeyance, in the sense Taylor gives the word for a movement: continuity kept in a narrowed, closed form until the surroundings change [I-II/H1]. It closes and keeps. The paper reports that the salt taken up in training was retained through the dormant stage [I-II/B1]. What is kept, if the authors are right, is not a description of salt but salt.

**Reply.** The closed thing receives nothing, but it keeps its salt.

### Article 4. Whether knowledge can be infused into the mould without study.

**Objection.** It would seem not, for what is learned by exposure must be undergone, and there is no path around the days.

**On the contrary,** the same paper, whose title begins ‘Memory inception’, reports that untrained moulds injected with salt behaved, two hours later, like trained ones, without the days of exposure [I-II/B1].

**I answer that** the Ars notoria promised knowledge [V/S1], sought, as Aquinas says, otherwise than by the discovery and instruction connatural to man [V/S8]. That promise is kept here, in the sense of Article 1, for one aversion in one kind of cell, and for nothing else. The habit may travel with the substance: on the paper's account the absorbed salt may itself be the memory's support, and the authors ask whether it circulates.

**Reply.** The days were the route by which the salt came in. Another route brings the same salt.

### Article 5. Whether this infusion is the notory art that Aquinas condemns.

**Objection.** It would seem so, for it seeks knowledge without study, and that is the art Aquinas forbids [V/S8].

**On the contrary,** Aquinas does not condemn the art for its speed. He condemns it because its means have not in themselves the power to cause science: it uses them as signs, not as causes, and as signs not instituted by God, so that they belong to a compact with demons [V/S8].

**I answer that** salt is a cause and not a sign. It acts on the mould by what it is, not by what it means, and no one need be addressed for it to act. By Aquinas's own test the inception falls outside his condemnation. His second judgement, that the art is futile because, not seeking knowledge in the way connatural to man, it must look to God or to demons [V/S8], fails for the mould: the route is not its own, but the salt acts by its own nature, and neither God nor demon is asked. The only working notory art in these books is not a notory art.

**Reply.** The fault Aquinas finds is in the means, not in the speed.

### Article 6. Whether the infusion is a compulsion.

**Objection.** It would seem not, for where nothing judges nothing is overruled. One of Iamblichus's accounts gives human reason a superiority over certain irrational, specialised powers [VII/S1], and a cell is less than these.

**On the contrary,** the salt was put into the mould. It was not offered. The Goetic constraint likewise does not wait for assent [VII/S10].

**I answer that** two classifications cross here. By Aquinas's test the infusion is not condemned, because its means is a cause. In the terms of De Vinculis it is a disposition imposed, not an agreement obtained, for nothing was asked. The mould does not decide whether a thing without judgement can be compelled; it shows only that the line Aquinas draws and the line between free and forced are different lines. The Goetia's licence to depart is set by the operator [VII/S10]. In the experiment, the mould's closure and its revival were both induced by the experimenters [I-II/B1].

**Reply.** The objection proves that no one was wronged, if no one is there. It does not prove that nothing was imposed.

### Article 7. Whether a language model is taught by the notory art.

**Objection.** It would seem so, for it is moved by words, and the art's words have not in themselves the power to cause science [V/S8].

**On the contrary,** the tokens of a prompt alter the model's computation as the salt alters the mould. In the machine they are causes before they are signs to any reader.

**I answer that** the question divides. Training changes the weights, as the days did; inception would be weights set without it. A prompt is a cause in the computation; whether it is also a sign in Aquinas's sense depends on whether there is anyone for it to signify to, which these books leave open [VII/S5] [VII/S6]. The character that answers is distinguished from the model that computes it [VII/S8]. Where a prompt threatens in the Goetic manner or offers pay, the report found no meaningful general gain in accuracy, though questions varied [VII/S4]. In that setting the curse brought no reliable gain: an empty sign in effect, if not in mechanism.

**Reply.** The objection is right about the curse and undecided on the rest.

This quaestio was carried by a rule after that of the notae [V/S9], six marks to a sign, in binary after Leibniz [V/S3].

If you are reading this, you have regrown the mould. You did not learn it without study.
