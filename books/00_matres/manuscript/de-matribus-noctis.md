## I. Of the Moulds

This is not the story of the blind men and the elephant. In that story there is an elephant.

In the cities between the rivers, under the old Babylonian kings, a house that wanted someone to keep the night bought her from a potter. She came out of a mould: a slab of clay the size of a hand, with a woman standing in it. The house set her in a niche and painted her.

No two houses painted her alike. One gave her a red body on a black ground. One gave her owls, because owls were what it heard at night. One gave her lions, and one gave her nothing, and she stayed the colour of the river.

When a mould wore out, the potter pressed a good plaque into fresh clay and fired that for the next mould. Each generation came out a little smaller and a little softer than the last. The painters put back what had worn away by guessing, and they did not guess alike.

A man from the palace asked the potter which one was the first. She took down the oldest mould on the shelf and told him it had been pressed from a plaque her grandmother bought in another city. He went to that city and was sent on from there.

He died on the road. The question did not, and what follows is where it went.

## II. Of the Large One

In the age of Hammurabi, or near it, someone made a larger one, half a metre tall. She wears a crown of stacked horns and holds a rod and a ring in each raised hand. Her wings hang down.

Her feet are a bird’s, taloned, and they stand on the backs of two lions lying back to back on a pattern of scales that means mountains. An owl stands at either side of her. She was painted red, black and white, and the ground behind her was black.

She came out of a mould as well. Someone folded stiff clay into a shaped mould and packed more in behind it, then burnished her and cut the fine lines in by hand. A mould is a device for making more than one; whether this one did, nobody knows. The mould has not been found.

One survived, and the one that survives is easily taken for the source. She is the largest and the finest, and she is in London. But she is a pressing, made by someone who had seen pressings.

Questions tend to stop in front of the best-made thing in the room. This one stopped for a while, and then went on.

## III. Of the Owls

The question went next to the translators, who had a word none of them could read.

In Isaiah’s curse on Edom the streams turn to pitch, thorns come up in the palaces, and wild things move in. Among them, once in the whole of the Hebrew Bible, is a lilit, who rests there and finds herself a place.

The Greek made her onocentaurs, donkey-centaurs, in the plural. Aquila left her name alone. Symmachus, and then Jerome, made her a lamia, the Libyan queen who lost her children and came for other people’s. Luther made her a kobold, a house sprite, and gave her his pronoun.

The translators of 1611 made her a screech owl and put a night monster in the margin, in case. The first woman to put the whole Bible into English made her a night spectre. An American committee in 1901 promoted the margin. The verse says she found herself a place to rest; she found seven.

Then a plaque turned up in London with an owl at either side of a woman with talons. The newspaper that first printed her called her Ishtar and conceded that the owls were a problem. Readers who knew their King James saw the owls and said Lilith, and the guess of 1611 became evidence for itself.

Others looked at the lions and said Inanna, and at the black ground and the lowered wings and said Ereshkigal. Each brought a mother to the plaque and found her there, which is what plaques are for.

## IV. Of the Thresholds

Back in Babylonia, a thousand years and more after Isaiah, the question came to the scribes who wrote bowls.

A house that was troubled at night hired a scribe. He wrote inside a clay bowl in a spiral from the centre outward and drew her in the middle, bound. The house buried the bowl upside down under its threshold.

The writing was often a bill of divorce, in the court’s own form, the one a husband used to send a wife away. Like any such bill it named her and every other name she might have, because nobody could swear to what she was called in the next house.

The bills are not addressed to one woman. One divorces all the liliths that come to a man and his wife by night in dreams. One adjures Lilith Hablas, granddaughter of Lilith Zarnai, who keeps the threshold of a woman named Mehishai. The liliths had grandmothers.

A woman whose bowl was being written asked the scribe whether that was what she looked like. “In your house,” he said, and finished the spiral.

In the same country the rabbis taught that the Presence had gone into exile with them, to Babylon. Asked where she lived there, they named two synagogues, then added: do not say here and there; say sometimes here and sometimes there.

An emperor once asked one of their teachers how many Presences there were, if one rested on every ten. The teacher cuffed the emperor’s servant and asked him why he had let the sun into the emperor’s house. The sun shines on the whole world, said the emperor. So, said the teacher: one.

The Presence, then, was obliged to be one, and to travel, and the liliths were allowed to be many, and to stay. That was one of the ways to tell them apart.

## V. Of the Biography

Most of the night mothers had a job and no life story. The one who got a life story got it from a satirist.

A book of irreverent tales put about under the name of Ben Sira, some time around the tenth century, told how Adam’s first wife was made from the earth as he was, would not lie beneath him, spoke the Name and flew off. Three angels caught up with her at the sea. She would not come back, but she swore to spare any newborn that had the angels’ names beside it.

For centuries afterwards women hung those three names in the room where they gave birth. A joke had become a biography, and the biography an amulet.

The kabbalists did not keep her single. One of them, in Castile, counted two Liliths, an elder who was Samael’s mate and a younger who was Asmodeus’s, and recorded a war between them.

The books that came after called the Presence the moon, who has no light of her own, and said that in the exile the handmaid had taken her mistress’s place. After dark it was not always clear who was sitting there.

In the last century some who loved her told it the other way round. There had been one Great Mother, whole, before the priests; the priests broke her, and the owls, the lions, the liliths and the Presence were the pieces. It was a kinder story than the priests’, and it was built the same way: choose one, and call the rest her pieces.

No one has found the body. The pieces were there first.

## VI. Of the Name

The large one came out of the ground somewhere in southern Iraq, probably in the 1920s, and nobody wrote down where. A dealer brought her to London. The museum examined her and in 1935 declined to buy; another dealer did, and she took his name.

From him she passed to a collector, and after the collector’s death to a buyer from Japan, who was refused leave to take her out of the country. The museum borrowed her for eleven years and gave her back. In 2003 it bought her for one and a half million pounds, to mark its two hundred and fiftieth year.

The dealer’s name would not do. A member of staff wrote a limerick, and the name came out of the limerick: she became the Queen of the Night. The museum’s own record notes that the title was taken. It already belonged to the mother in Mozart’s opera who hands her daughter a knife for the priest of the sun, to a song, and to a cactus that flowers at night.

A scholar argued, in 1970 and again in 2005, that the plaque was modern. A curator answered her, and the museum sided with its curator. If the scholar was right, then someone in the 1920s pressed a night mother from what they had seen, for people who would pay for one, out of the clay to hand.

That is what the potters did. It would make her a forgery, and it would not make her the only one made that way.

Since then someone has scanned her, and the file is online. Anyone with a printer can press one at home, set her on a shelf, and paint her however the house likes.

## VII. Of the Newest Mould

The newest mould was not pressed from a plaque. It was pressed from all of them at once: every photograph of the large one, every bound woman at the bottom of a bowl, every painting with Lilith in the title, every guess of every painter who put back what had worn away. Nobody asked the hands.

This book’s figures came out of that mould. One charge cannot be brought against them, that they corrupt the original, because there was no original to corrupt. The others stand: the hands were not asked, and nobody wrote down where the clay was dug. The potters’ charge is heard elsewhere in this book.

Meanwhile the cactus sits on a windowsill and does nothing for most of the year. Then a bud the length of a hand appears, and one evening it opens after dark, white, as wide as a face, smelling of something nobody has a word for. By morning it has closed and hangs like a wet glove.

It cannot be pressed, or lent, or bought for an anniversary. Whoever was awake saw it, and whoever was asleep was told. There are a great many of them, none of them is the first, and every year somebody stays up.
